Journey design

Four bases. Two mountain days. Cities explored on foot.

The trip has a geographic progression: an Appalachian ridge, a river-built metropolis, a fortified colonial capital, then the St. Lawrence landscapes of Charlevoix. Montréal and Québec City are walking bases; the car is mainly for transfers and nature days.

Act I · The mountains first

Franconia is a weather window, not a fixed appointment.

Franconia is weather-dependent, but the schedule does not create two equally comfortable ridge days. Aug. 27 is the primary ridge opportunity. Aug. 28 is only a second attempt if everyone is rested, conditions are clearly favorable, and you knowingly accept a late international transfer to Montréal. Otherwise, use Aug. 28 for a lower-elevation Notch morning and transfer north.

Weather-window rule: Aug. 27 is the primary Franconia Ridge day. Hike only if high-summit wind, thunderstorms, visibility and group fatigue are all favorable. If not, do Flume Gorge + The Basin. Treat Aug. 28 as a conditional second-chance ridge day, not a promise: only attempt it if everyone is rested and willing to reach Montréal late after a strenuous hike.

Franconia Ridge Loop

7–9 hr hikeTrail access: free~8.9 miWeather-dependent

Route: Falling Waters Trail → Little Haystack → Mount Lincoln → Mount Lafayette → Greenleaf Trail → Old Bridle Path. The ridge is resistant bedrock left standing above valleys deepened by Pleistocene ice. The walk moves from stream-cut forest to krummholz and then onto an exposed alpine spine where weather, thin soils and wind control what can grow.

On the ridge, spend less time chasing summit photos and more time reading the landforms: U-shaped valleys, cirques, steep headwalls and the sharp contrast between protected forest and the treeless crest. That vocabulary will return later in Jacques-Cartier and Hautes-Gorges.

○ Worth doing Flume Gorge

1½ hrPaid · $18 online adultReserve time slot

The boardwalk enters an 800-foot slot where Flume Brook exploits joints in Conway granite. The walls rise roughly 70–90 feet and narrow to about 12 feet. Water and freeze-thaw keep widening fractures that were already present in the rock.

Perfect bad-ridge-weather substitute. NH State Parks lists the full self-guided loop at about two miles and 1½ hours; online reservations save money and protect entry.

+ Bonus The Basin

30–60 minFree

The Pemigewasset River spins sand, gravel and cobbles through a bedrock hollow, polishing a large pothole into the granite. It is a small-scale demonstration of abrasion by sediment-laden water—easy to see because the erosional tool and the resulting form are in the same frame.

Skip it if the group is tired or you need to protect the Montréal transfer.

ParkingLafayette Place is the practical trailhead for Falling Waters/Old Bridle Path. Arrive very early; summer parking fills. Do not count on roadside improvisation.
FoodCarry a full hiking lunch, water and salty snacks. Dinner in walkable downtown Lincoln; keep it uncomplicated after the ridge.
BookingFranconia Ridge itself is not a ticketed attraction. Flume Gorge should be booked online for a timed arrival.

Magog waterfront + Lake Memphrémagog

60–90 min walkFree

Walk from the downtown waterfront toward MacPherson Wharf and the mouth of the Magog River. Lake Memphrémagog crosses the international boundary: most of the lake lies in Québec, while much of its watershed reaches south into Vermont. You have just driven through the same upland basin that feeds it.

Look back toward the Appalachian hills and compare them with Franconia. The relief is lower and rounder here, but the continuity is the point: the political border interrupts the map, not the mountain-and-water system.

Downtown Magog + lunch

60–90 minLunch

Use rue Principale for lunch, then cross back toward the lake rather than turning the stop into a shopping block. Magog grew where the lake drains into the Magog River; water, transport and later industry made this more than a resort town.

This is a compact first reading of southern Québec: a French-speaking town whose geography is tied to a lake and watershed shared with New England.

Act II · Montréal from the inside

Live in the Plateau; visit downtown.

Use the Plateau and Mile End to understand Montréal as a lived city of dense 19th- and early-20th-century housing, immigration, neighborhood commerce and industrial conversion. Old Montréal then supplies the earlier colonial and mercantile layers beneath it.

Saturday · August 29

Plateau → Mile End → Jean-Talon

A nearly car-free day of architecture, alleys, bagels, cafés and Montréal’s great food market.

MODERATE: long walking day, mostly flat
Sleep · Montréal
Mostly walkingPlateau → Mile End ~20–30 min wanderingMile End → Jean-Talon ~25 min walk

Square Saint-Louis, Plateau streets & ruelles vertes

2–3 hr wanderingFree

Walk the classic Montréal fabric rather than a checklist: colorful duplexes and triplexes, exterior staircases, mature street trees, small parks and resident-made green alleys. The Plateau has roughly 120 green alleys—social spaces created by residents, not themed tourist installations.

This is how the city explains itself. Montréal’s personality lives as much in these semi-private back lanes and densely inhabited streets as in its monuments.

Mile End + St-Viateur / Fairmount bagel comparison

1½–2 hrFood purchase

Mile End mixes long-established Jewish food traditions with music, design, cafés and independent retail. Buy one hot sesame bagel from each of the rival institutions rather than treating either as a formal meal.

After the Plateau’s residential form, Mile End shows how immigration and neighborhood-scale commerce created Montréal’s most recognizable food culture.

Jean-Talon Market

1–1½ hrFree to enterPay for food

One of North America’s large open-air markets, overflowing in late August with Québec produce. Use it as a tasting stop: berries, tomatoes, breads, cheeses, preserves and prepared foods rather than a rushed photo stop.

Your dates land at the peak of Québec’s short growing season, when the market makes the province’s agricultural abundance visible.

LunchGraze through Mile End and Jean-Talon rather than booking a sit-down lunch.

★ Essential Place d’Armes + Notre-Dame Basilica

45–60 minPaid admissionAdvance ticket sensible

Sunday timing: current published sightseeing hours begin at 12:30 p.m., so use the morning for Old Montréal streets / Place d’Armes and enter the Basilica after 12:30. The Basilica is Montréal’s theatrical religious interior: Gothic Revival architecture, deep blue vaults and carved ornament. Outside, Place d’Armes compresses several generations of the city’s architecture into one square.

This is the point where French-Catholic identity, British-era commercial wealth and modern Montréal overlap spatially.

★ Essential Pointe-à-Callière

1½–2 hrAdult $30

A museum built over the archaeological layers of Montréal’s founding site. You descend physically through the city’s early footprint instead of seeing history as objects removed from place.

This is the urban counterpart to the geology days: layers underfoot explain why the city exists where it does.

○ Worth doing Mount Royal + Kondiaronk Belvedere

2 hrFree

A wooded climb to the classic overlook, where downtown rises below the mountain and the St. Lawrence spreads beyond it. The mountain gives Montréal its name and still organizes the city’s geography.

After seeing the old port from street level, the overlook makes the whole city legible in one view.

Optional substitution: If you prefer industrial history to another long walk, replace part of Mount Royal with Atwater Market + the Lachine Canal. The canal was central to Montréal’s rise as an industrial city.

+ Bonus Atwater Market + Lachine Canal

1½–2½ hrFree to walkMarket purchases

The 1933 Art Deco market sits beside the historic canal in Little Burgundy. Walk west or east along the water through former industrial districts now converted into parks, housing and food destinations.

Old Montréal explains the mercantile city; the Lachine Canal explains the industrial city.

Act III · The St. Lawrence and New France

Québec City as a four-night hub.

Québec exists where the St. Lawrence narrows beneath Cap Diamant. That geography explains the port, the split between Lower and Upper Town, the walls, the Citadelle and the battles fought for the city. Read the old city from river level upward.

Tuesday · September 1

Fortress, empire and the river

This is the key history day. Walk from the topography of power down into the oldest commercial streets, then see the whole cliff from the water at sunset.

MODERATE–HARD: hills, stairs, cobbles and several hours on foot
Sleep · Québec City
Saint-Jean-Baptiste → walls ~10–15 min walkAll principal stops on foot

★ Essential Citadelle + Plains of Abraham

2–2½ hrCitadelle admissionGuided tour sensible

Start on Cap Diamant, more than 90 metres above the river. The cliff protected Québec on the river side; the open plateau to the west was the vulnerable approach, which is why fortifications concentrated there. The present Citadelle was largely built by the British from 1819 to 1832, while the Plains of Abraham preserve the terrain of the 1759 battle that broke the French hold on Québec.

Stand first at the edge and orient yourself: river below, Lévis opposite, Lower Town squeezed between cliff and water, open ground to the west. The military history becomes much easier to understand once the topography is visible.

Dufferin Terrace + Château Frontenac

45–60 minTerrace free

Dufferin Terrace occupies the crown of Cap Diamant above the river narrows. Château Frontenac is the obvious landmark, but the enduring fact is the headland: whoever held this height could watch shipping, defend the anchorage and command the connection between Upper and Lower Town.

the river constriction, Lévis opposite, the Lower Town directly below and the line of defensive high ground toward the Citadelle.

Saint-Louis Forts & Châteaux

45–60 minAdmission free during your datesGuided program may cost extra

Beneath Dufferin Terrace are the archaeological remains of the forts, residences and administrative seat used by French and then British governors from the 17th century into the 19th. You are not viewing artifacts detached from their setting; you are walking through the buried footprint of colonial government on the same defensible headland.

The glamorous boardwalk above becomes more meaningful when you understand that the colonial government of a vast territory operated on this exact footprint. Parks Canada admission is free through Sept. 7, 2026.

Fortifications → Petit-Champlain → Place Royale

2½–3 hrStreets/walls free

Follow the military edge of Upper Town, then descend into the intimate stone streets of Lower Town. Place Royale puts you close to the city's 17th-century origins; Petit-Champlain compresses the cliff, river and commercial city into a few lanes.

Do not rush through at midday. Return or linger toward evening as day-trip crowds thin.

Québec–Lévis ferry at sunset

45–75 min round tripC$4.25 each way adult 16–64

The crossing takes you away from the cliff just far enough to see the entire composition: Lower Town at water level, the fortified bluff above, and Château Frontenac anchoring the skyline.

It is the cheapest “harbor cruise” in the city and the best way to understand Québec as a river fortress rather than simply a collection of old streets.

FoodLunch in Lower Town; dinner at Le Don Végane or L’Orygine.
WalkingExpect hills, stairs and cobbles. The funicular is a paid shortcut if desired.
BookingBook dinner. Saint-Louis general admission is free during the Canada Strong Pass period; check guided-tour availability.

★ Essential Huron-Wendat Museum & Wendake

2–3 hrMuseum paidGuided experiences: reserve

Begin with the Musée Huron-Wendat and, if available, a guided program or the Ekionkiestha’ longhouse. The permanent exhibition is presented by a national institution of the Huron-Wendat Nation and links objects, oral history, diplomacy, territory and contemporary identity. More than half of the Nation still lives in Wendake.

Listen for the geography of relationships: the St. Lawrence, trade routes, alliances and movement between communities. This history is not a preface to New France; it is one of the political worlds New France entered and depended upon.

+ Optional Parc national de la Jacques-Cartier

4–5 hrSépaq park admissionBuy access online

Revised role: Treat Jacques-Cartier as optional. If you want more Québec City history, return after Wendake for the Plains, museums, neighborhoods or a relaxed city afternoon instead. If you choose the park, use a preselected moderate route rather than deciding at the trailhead. A broad U-shaped glacial valley cut deeply into the Laurentian plateau, with the Jacques-Cartier River winding along the floor. Choose a moderate trail or riverside walk rather than chasing maximum elevation—you already have Franconia and still have Hautes-Gorges ahead.

Franconia showed an alpine ridge; Jacques-Cartier shows the work of continental ice at valley scale.

ParkingDrive from the Québec City base. Use official parking at Wendake and the Sépaq visitor area.

Thursday · September 3

Montmorency + Île d’Orléans

The St. Lawrence turns from military geography into agricultural geography: waterfall, old parishes, farms, orchards and village roads.

MODERATE: waterfall stairs/paths + a relaxed driving circuit with short village stops
Sleep · Québec City

Montmorency Falls

1½–2 hrC$13.90 regular adult accessCable car extra C$14.95 round tripBuy access online

The Montmorency River drops 83 metres where the Laurentian upland meets the St. Lawrence lowland. View the falls from below first, then cross above: the abrupt escarpment is the important landform, not simply the height of the water.

A short, vertical geological event before the slow horizontal landscapes of Île d’Orléans.

Île d’Orléans circuit

4½–6 hrRoad/villages freeFood & museum purchases

Default circuit: Sainte-Pétronille → Saint-Laurent → Saint-Jean → one farm/orchard → Sainte-Famille → Cassis Monna. As you drive, watch the long narrow lots running back from the road and river. This ribbon-like cadastral pattern gave many farms access to the St. Lawrence and organized settlement around parish villages, a durable imprint of the seigneurial landscape.

Treat the island as a cultural landscape. Stop where you can see field boundaries, church spires, old houses and the river in one view; those relationships matter more than accumulating farm shops.

ParkingUse official Montmorency parking, then drive the island. Village stops are short and informal.
Food stopsCassis Monna & Filles plus farm stands; buy picnic ingredients as you go.

Act IV · Impact crater to glacial gorge

Charlevoix is not a detour. It is the finale.

Charlevoix sits within a roughly 54-km-wide ancient impact structure centered near Mont des Éboulements. Much later, glaciers and rivers reworked that fractured terrain. The result is an unusual meeting of impact geology, steep Laurentian relief, farms and the St. Lawrence.

Baie-Saint-Paul

1½–2 hrFree to wander

A compact arts town between mountain walls and the St. Lawrence lowlands. Its galleries, old streets and food culture make it a rare place where the evening base is itself worth exploring on foot.

It preserves the hub-and-spoke philosophy. You unpack once for two nights and can walk to dinner instead of ending a scenic day in an anonymous roadside property.

Route 362 · the St. Lawrence Route

3–4 hr with stopsFree scenic drive

Route 362 repeatedly climbs from river level onto high agricultural benches and drops again toward the St. Lawrence. Do not drive it as a sequence of viewpoints. Watch how roads, farms and villages negotiate unusually steep relief, especially around Les Éboulements and Saint-Irénée.

The road is the interpretation. Each descent reveals the vertical relationship between upland farms, escarpment and river; each climb shows how settlement occupies the limited gentler surfaces.

★ Essential Les Éboulements + Saint-Joseph-de-la-Rive

1–1½ hr combinedFree

Les Éboulements lies near the center of the Charlevoix impact structure. The ancient collision shattered and displaced bedrock on a regional scale; later erosion and glaciation sculpted the relief you see now. From the high village, descend to Saint-Joseph-de-la-Rive at water level to feel that relief rather than merely reading about it.

If the Observatoire de l’Astroblème has a program that fits your date, prioritize it over another gallery or shop. The point of this afternoon is to recognize the landscape as a geological structure, then see how roads and villages inhabit it.

ParkingChoose lodging in the center with included parking. Leave the car there for the evening.
FoodBaie-Saint-Paul is ideal for a bakery/produce picnic supply run before Hautes-Gorges and a relaxed dinner tonight.
Do not addDo not force Tadoussac or the Eastern Townships into this version. They would dilute the finale.

HARD if Acropole; MODERATE with valley alternative
Sleep · Baie-Saint-Paul

Hautes-Gorges valley

Full daySépaq park admissionBuy access / check shuttle details

The Rivière Malbaie occupies a deep glacial trough cut into the Laurentian plateau. At valley level the slopes rise as walls; from higher viewpoints the river becomes a narrow line inside a much larger U-shaped system. Ice established the large form, while the modern river now works within the valley floor.

Compare this directly with Franconia: there you walked the exposed crest between valleys; here you begin inside the trough and climb toward its rim. The geology is related in process but completely different in spatial experience.
